NFL Sunday Ticket Alternative: Is IPTV Worth It?

Sunday Ticket isn't expensive because games are being withheld from you, the way a regional blackout works on other leagues. It's expensive because it's the only legal source for something a lot of fans want, and the NFL prices that scarcity accordingly.

Paying for exclusivity, not for extra games

Sunday Ticket is built for one job: every out-of-market NFL game on Sunday afternoons, in one app. It runs $350 to $450 for the season depending on which provider bundles it, with pricing shifting year to year, so treat those as approximate. It doesn't include Sunday night, Monday night, or Thursday night football, since those already air on national broadcast and cable networks, and it doesn't touch anything outside the NFL. That price tag isn't padded with extras, it's what exclusivity costs when there's no other legal way to get the same coverage.

For a fan who specifically wants every out-of-market Sunday game and is otherwise happy with their current setup, that's a fair trade. The friction shows up for people already running two or three other services just to cover the rest of what they watch during the week, where a $400 season add-on lands on top of bills that already cover plenty.

The Sunday problem IPTV actually solves

Sunday football has a scheduling quirk most other leagues don't deal with the same way: the early and late afternoon windows each pack in several games at once, so a household wanting two games running at the same time, one in the living room, one somewhere else, needs devices that can keep up. True picture-in-picture across two live streams isn't something most streaming hardware handles cleanly, so in practice the realistic version of watching two games is fast, reliable channel switching rather than a split screen. That's a separate issue from Sunday Ticket's pricing, but it's worth knowing either way, since it shapes what device setup actually matters on a Sunday more than the service itself does.

When paying for exclusivity still makes sense

If every out-of-market Sunday game genuinely matters to you and you're already satisfied with the rest of your TV setup, paying the premium for that exclusivity is a reasonable choice, there's no better legal source for exactly that coverage. It stops making sense once you're paying $400 for games you'd only catch occasionally, on top of several other services already covering everything else you watch. The honest test is to count how many out-of-market Sunday games you actually sat down and watched last season rather than could have watched, since those two numbers are usually a lot further apart than people expect.
