How Much Does It Cost to Watch NHL Without Cable?

"Cut cable, save money" sounds simple until you're three streaming apps deep trying to cover a full regular season and playoff run. Here's an honest look at what each path actually costs once everything's added up, not just the headline price.

What cable actually costs, beyond the base price

Cable pricing rarely stops at the number advertised. Equipment rental for a cable box, regional sports network fees, broadcast fees, and taxes all get added on top of the base package, and a lot of that detail only shows up once you're already a customer and looking closely at a bill. Promotional pricing is also common, which means the price you signed up for often isn't the price you'll be paying a year in.

What a streaming-only approach actually costs

The appeal of "just stream it" is real, but NHL coverage specifically tends to be split across more than one app or service over a season: one for national broadcasts, a separate out-of-market sports add-on, and a different service again for everything that isn't hockey. Priced individually, each one can look affordable. Added together across a long regular season and an extended playoff run, a stack of three or four separate monthly charges often lands closer to a cable bill than people expect, and now you're also juggling several different apps and logins just to follow the season.

Where IPTV fits into the cost picture

An IPTV subscription is priced as one monthly or term-based cost that includes NHL coverage inside the Sports category, alongside the rest of your live channels and a Movies & TV Shows library. Rather than comparing it to cable's base price or to one streaming app's price in isolation, the fair comparison is one IPTV subscription against the full stack of whatever you'd otherwise need to piece together to cover the same ground, while also confirming your specific team's regional coverage upfront.

Hidden costs people forget to count

  • Promotional pricing that expires. A low first-year rate that jumps afterward is common with cable and some streaming bundles alike. Ask what the price looks like after any introductory period.
  • Equipment and rental fees. Cable boxes, DVR rental, and sometimes installation fees add up outside the advertised monthly rate.
  • Multiple devices or households. If you need more than one screen running at once, check whether that's included or costs extra, regardless of which service you choose.
  • Data usage, if you're on a connection with a data cap, since streaming video uses considerably more data than cable, which matters more across a full season plus playoffs than a shorter stretch.

A realistic example of how the math plays out

Picture a typical setup: a base streaming service for general TV, a separate out-of-market sports package specifically for NHL games, and a third subscription to cover movies and shows the first two don't carry. Priced individually, each one might look reasonable on its own, $10 here, $15 there. Added together as a monthly total, plus whatever device or platform fees come with any of them, the stack frequently ends up close to or above what a single consolidated subscription would cost, while still requiring you to manage three separate logins, three separate billing dates, and three separate apps just to catch one night of hockey.

Does switching actually save everyone money?

Not necessarily, and it's worth being honest about that. If you already watch very little TV outside of hockey season, or you're genuinely happy juggling two apps because each one is cheap on its own, the savings from consolidating may be smaller than expected. Where the savings show up clearly is for households already paying for three or more separate services to cover sports, general TV, and movies, where one subscription replacing that whole stack tends to make the biggest visible difference across a long season, both in total cost and in how much simpler the setup becomes.

A simple way to actually compare

The clearest way to compare options isn't headline price against headline price. Write down everything you currently watch across a typical week, hockey included, then price out what it would genuinely take to replicate that under each option: cable with its full real cost, a streaming-only stack covering every app you'd need, and a single IPTV subscription.
