How Much Does It Cost to Watch NFL, NBA, MLB & NHL Without Cable?

"Cut cable, save money" sounds simple until you add up what following a league actually costs across cable, that league's own streaming package, and everything else you watch. Below are real, typical price ranges for all four major leagues, what each one's package actually covers, what to check before you switch, and where an IPTV subscription like TruWave TV fits in. Treat every figure here as a typical range, since exact pricing shifts year to year and by provider.

What cable actually costs, beyond the base price

A basic cable or streaming-TV bundle that includes sports channels has generally run somewhere in the $70 to $90 a month range for a lot of providers, and that's often before equipment rental, regional sports network fees, broadcast fees, and taxes get added on top. Promotional pricing is also common, which means the price you signed up for often isn't the price you'll be paying a year in.

Switching delivery method, cable to streaming or the reverse, doesn't change those rights agreements. Confirm with any provider which regional sports network they actually carry before assuming your team's games are included.

What to actually check before you commit, by league

Cost isn't the only thing worth testing before a season starts. What actually reveals whether a service holds up varies by sport:

  • NFL: almost the entire week's national demand lands in a few windows, mainly Sunday afternoon. Test a stream during an actual Sunday slate, not a quiet weeknight, since that's the hour that shows whether a service can handle it.
  • NBA: no league schedules as many back-to-backs. Test how fast an app finds tonight's game after last night's, since a slow guide or search is a bigger problem over 82 games than it looks on paper.
  • MLB: 162 games over six months means the real test is consistency, not a single big night. Pay attention to an ordinary Tuesday game as much as a marquee one, and confirm the app updates and resumes reliably game after game.
  • NHL: picture quality during fast action matters more here than almost anywhere else. Watch a full period rather than a clip, and judge a stream during an actual rush up the ice, not a stoppage in play.

Across all four, confirm regional coverage specifically, by network name, rather than accepting a general answer like "sports are included". Confirm device compatibility with whatever you're already using to watch. And confirm support is actually reachable live, not only during weekday business hours, since a bad connection during a game doesn't wait until Monday morning to get fixed.

Hidden costs people forget to count

  • Promotional pricing that expires: a low first-year rate that jumps afterward is common with cable and some streaming bundles alike.
  • Equipment and rental fees: cable boxes, DVR rental, and sometimes installation fees add up outside the advertised monthly rate.
  • Regional blackouts: every league package above still runs into them for your own team's home games.
  • Multiple devices or households: if you need more than one screen running at once, check whether that's included or costs extra, regardless of which service you choose.

Does switching actually save everyone money?

Not necessarily, and it's worth being honest about that. If you already watch very little TV outside one league's season, or you specifically need a league's out-of-market package and are happy paying for it directly, the savings from switching may be smaller than the example above. Where the savings show up clearly is for households already paying for a general TV bundle plus one or more league packages plus a separate movie service, where one subscription replacing that whole stack makes the biggest visible difference.

Frequently asked questions

Is it actually cheaper to watch sports without cable?

It can be, especially if you currently stack a cable bundle plus a league-specific package plus a separate movie service. That combination often runs $105 to $135 a month depending on the league, while a single IPTV subscription covering sports and movies together typically costs a fraction of that. The savings come from consolidating, not from streaming by itself.

How much do NFL Sunday Ticket, NBA League Pass, MLB.TV, and NHL's out-of-market package cost?

Roughly, per season: Sunday Ticket $350 to $450, full NBA League Pass $150 to $200 (a single team pass closer to $120), MLB.TV $140 to $150, and an out-of-market NHL package $150 to $180. All of these change year to year and by provider, and none of them replace a base TV subscription for national and regional broadcasts.

Do these league packages get rid of blackout restrictions?

No. League-specific streaming packages are mainly for out-of-market games. Your own team's home-market broadcasts are usually still tied to a regional sports network and require a separate cable or streaming subscription that carries it, regardless of which delivery method you use.
