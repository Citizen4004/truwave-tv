Best Device to Watch MLB Games

The right device for watching MLB games on IPTV depends on what you already own, but for baseball specifically it also depends on how well a device holds up across 162 games played night after night for six months. Below are the main options, what actually changes between them for a season this long, and how to set each one up.

What actually matters for a 162-game season

Baseball is unusual among the major sports because of sheer volume: most teams play close to every day for six months, and a meaningful share of those games run past three hours once extra innings or a slow pitching duel gets involved. That changes what "best device" means. A device that handles one exciting Sunday afternoon game just fine can behave differently after being left paused mid-game for a bathroom break, or after running the same app every night for a month straight. For baseball, the two things worth paying attention to are how a device handles a single long session without the stream degrading, and how cleanly it wakes back up and reloads a channel after sitting idle, rather than how fast or expensive the device is on paper.

Firestick

Amazon's Fire TV Stick is still the cheapest way into IPTV, and for most of the season it holds up fine. The thing worth knowing for baseball specifically is that entry-level Firestick models have less memory to spare, so leaving a stream app sitting idle during a pitching change or a rain delay for too long can cause it to get closed in the background, meaning you come back to a frozen screen or a channel that needs reloading. A newer Fire TV Stick with more onboard memory handles this far better across a six-month season than the cheapest model on the shelf.

Android TV boxes

A dedicated Android TV box generally has more RAM than a Firestick, and that matters more over 162 nightly games than it would for a sport played a couple times a week. The extra memory means the streaming app is less likely to get force-closed during a slow half-inning, and the box tends to resume a paused game faster than a stick does. If you're watching almost every night for months, this is where a box starts to pay for itself over a stick.

Smart TV apps

A built-in smart TV app removes a device from the equation entirely, and the app's sleep and wake behavior is tied to the TV's own power management rather than a separate box's memory limits, which tends to make it reliable for the same long nightly habit. The tradeoff is that update frequency and feature support vary more by TV brand than with a dedicated streaming device, so a three-year-old smart TV may lag behind a new Firestick in app updates even if it holds a stream open just as well. Check our Devices page for which smart TV platforms are currently supported.

Phones and tablets

TruWave TV's app also runs on Android and iOS phones, which matters most for baseball in two specific situations: catching an afternoon game while you're out somewhere, since day games are common during the season, and having a backup ready for the nights your main TV setup acts up. Phones are more prone to the operating system closing a backgrounded app to save battery than a TV-connected device is, so they're better treated as a backup or on-the-go option across the season than as your everyday setup for watching from the couch.

What to prioritize if you're buying new hardware

If you're deciding what to buy specifically for a 162-game season, prioritize in this order: a stable internet connection first, since that affects every device equally, then a device with enough memory to keep a streaming app loaded through a long game without getting closed in the background, then wired Ethernet if your setup makes that realistic. A mid-range box or stick that stays stable through a slow Tuesday night game will serve you better across six months than a flashier device that occasionally needs a restart mid-broadcast.

Setting up for a doubleheader or group viewing

Day-night doubleheaders and getaway-day scheduling come up more in baseball than in most sports, and running back-to-back games on one device is mostly a question of whether the app stays open cleanly between them rather than needing a fresh login each time. If you're hosting rather than watching alone, a single large screen with a strong connection still beats multiple smaller devices spread around a room. If the plan is to run the game in more than one room at once, check your plan's simultaneous-connection limit ahead of time. See our multi-device guide for how connection limits work.

Frequently asked questions

What's the best device for watching MLB games on IPTV?

There isn't one universal answer, but for a 162-game season the devices that hold a stream open reliably through long games and resume cleanly after a pause, generally a smart TV app or a dedicated Android TV box, tend to hold up better over six months than the cheapest entry-level stick.

Can I watch MLB games on my phone with IPTV?

Yes, TruWave TV's app runs on Android and iOS phones, which is useful for catching a day game while you're out or as a backup if your main TV setup has an issue.

Do I need a powerful device to stream MLB games smoothly?

Not an especially powerful one. What matters more for baseball is how well a device holds a stream open through a full three-hour-plus game and wakes back up cleanly after being paused, since raw processing power rarely causes buffering on a modern streaming box.

What devices does TruWave TV work on?

Should I use a wired connection for a long season?

If your device and router support it, yes, but for baseball specifically the bigger factor is often which device keeps the streaming app loaded in memory between games rather than reloading it from scratch every night across a 162-game season.

Can I switch devices without losing my subscription?

Yes, your IPTV subscription isn't tied to one device. You can install the app on a new device and log in with the same credentials.

What's the best setup for a doubleheader or watch party?
